The Market for Lemons

When one side of a transaction knows more than the other, markets can work poorly. Watch a used-car market unravel when quality is hidden, then see how information fixes it.

Part 1 · Watch a market unravel

100 used cars, from a worst “lemon” (1) to a best (100). A car’s quality is worth that many hundred dollars to its owner; a buyer would value it 30% higher, so every sale would create gains for both sides. Sellers know their own car’s quality. Buyers can see only the average quality of cars on the market, and pay for an average car.

A stylized version of the “market for lemons” model: sellers sell only if the price is at least their car’s value, and buyers pay 1.3 times the average quality they expect.

Part 2 · Signal or screen?

Parties can reduce information gaps. Sellers send signals, such as warranties and credentials; buyers screen, using tests and questions.

Part 3 · Adverse selection or moral hazard?

Adverse selection occurs when hidden characteristics lead to a poor mix of participants. Moral hazard occurs when protection from risk changes behavior.

Ethics and information. Good business ethics require that all parties receive complete information before a transaction. Withholding what you know about a product may be profitable for a while, but it harms buyers and, as the market above shows, it can destroy the market itself.
