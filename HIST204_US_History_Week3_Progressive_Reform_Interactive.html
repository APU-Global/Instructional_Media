Week 3 Interactive: Muckrakers and Progressive Reform

Week 3 objective: Connect the problems that Progressive Era reformers exposed to the reforms that citizens and governments adopted between 1887 and 1920.

The Progressive pattern

Many Progressive campaigns followed the same path: journalists exposed a problem, activists such as settlement-house workers, women's clubs, and social gospel ministers organized, and governments passed reforms. Theodore Roosevelt labeled the journalists who exposed business practices, poverty, and corruption "muckrakers."

Planning your Visualize a Historical Document infographic?

Use the same chain: the problem (context), the document (content and purpose), and the reform or change it helped produce (impact and legacy). For example: Upton Sinclair wrote The Jungle (1906) to reveal the exploitation of meatpacking workers and build support for socialism. Instead, its major impact was to lay bare industrial food production, and it led to the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act in 1906. A document's impact is not always what its author intended.

Takeaway: Progressive reform usually began with exposure and ended with law: muckrakers and activists made problems visible, and antitrust laws, the 1906 food and drug laws, housing reform, and the Nineteenth Amendment answered them. As you read a Progressive Era source, ask which problem it exposed, whose voices it included, and what changed because of it.
