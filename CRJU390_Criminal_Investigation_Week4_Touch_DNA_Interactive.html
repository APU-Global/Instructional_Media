Week 4 Interactive: Touch DNA and Forensic Error

Week 4 objective: Predict when touch DNA is most likely to survive and classify the kinds of forensic errors NIJ found in wrongful convictions.

What the research found

McKee describes touch DNA as the skin cells left behind when a person touches an object, collected with a slightly moistened swab. He warns that DNA degrades from heat, humidity, sunlight, and microbes, so biological items go in breathable packaging such as paper bags to prevent moisture buildup. In NIJ-funded tests on steel bolts and cotton fabric, the amount of DNA decreased over time, UV light did the most damage (UV-exposed samples produced no profiles), and DNA degraded more in high temperature and low humidity. Investigators are more likely to recover usable DNA in cool, dry indoor settings than in hot, humid outdoor ones, and from stainless steel than from fabric.

A separate NIJ review of 732 wrongful-conviction cases sorted forensic errors into five types: (1) forensic science reports, (2) individualization or classification, (3) testimony, (4) officer of the court, and (5) evidence handling and reporting.
