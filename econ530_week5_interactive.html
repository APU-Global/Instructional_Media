One Product, Different Prices

Price discrimination means charging different customers different prices for the same product when costs do not differ. Test when it works, how much profit it adds, who gains access, and where fairness and the law set limits.

Part 1 · Price the product

A software firm sells a design tool to two customer groups with different willingness to pay: students (price-sensitive) and professionals. Each extra copy costs $2 to serve. Choose a pricing system and set the price.

Shading: dark = the firm’s profit, light = customers’ surplus (what buyers would have paid beyond the price). Student demand: Q = 60 − 6P. Professional demand: Q = 60 − 2P. Invented for practice. Price discrimination requires market power and a way to prevent resale.

Part 3 · Where are the limits?

Fairness and reputation. Discriminatory pricing can raise profit and expand access, as students gain here, and it can also seem unfair. Customers who learn they paid more may leave.
Law and ethics. Some forms of discrimination are illegal, such as pricing based on protected characteristics. Evaluate effects on customers and on long-term reputation, and reflect on how your values shape what you consider fair.
