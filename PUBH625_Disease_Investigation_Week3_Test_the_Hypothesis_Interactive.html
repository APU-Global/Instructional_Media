Week 3 Interactive: Hypotheses in an Outbreak Investigation

Week 3 objective: Distinguish how investigators generate and test hypotheses, and judge whether an association is likely to be real.

What you need to know

Many steps happen at once. An investigation includes case definitions, confirming cases, the background rate, case finding, descriptive epidemiology, generating and testing hypotheses, environmental samples, control measures, and communication with the public. Many occur at the same time.
Generating hypotheses. Ideas come from the epidemic curve and who is ill, from what is known about earlier outbreaks, and from open-ended interviews about everything ill people ate or did.
Testing hypotheses. An analytic study, often a case-control study, compares exposures among ill and well people. Each exposure is a separate hypothesis.
Significant is not the same as true. At p < 0.05, a chance finding happens 5% of the time. Check the size of the association and its confidence interval, biologic plausibility, dose-response, timing against the incubation period, and how many cases the exposure explains.

Takeaway: An investigation earns its conclusion: generate hypotheses widely, test them with an analytic study, and weigh significance alongside effect size, plausibility, timing, and how many cases the exposure explains. Resist pressure to name a source too early.
