Week 4 Interactive: Community Event-Based Surveillance

Week 4 objective: Recognize the trigger events used in Sierra Leone’s community surveillance and calculate how well a surveillance system performs.

What you need to know

Why it was built. In late 2014, many people with Ebola were found only after they died, which prolonged transmission. Community event-based surveillance (CEBS) trained community members to report events in their own villages.
Six trigger events. More than two sick or dead household members; sickness or death after an unsafe burial or corpse washing; a sick or dead health worker or traditional healer; a sick or dead traveler; a sick or dead contact of a patient; an unsafe burial or corpse washing. A seventh category, “other,” covered unusual events.
What happened. Of 12,126 alerts, 95% were logged as “other,” mostly deaths in the community. CEBS found 16 of 53 confirmed cases (sensitivity 30%), with a positive predictive value of 6%.
Why it still mattered. In Kambia District, CEBS detected unlinked cases 1 to 3 days after symptom onset, versus 5 and 7 days for other parts of the system, and monitors also found measles clusters.

Practice 2: Measure a surveillance system

Ratnayake et al. (2016) measured sensitivity as confirmed cases found by CEBS divided by all confirmed cases found by the whole national system, and positive predictive value (PPV) as confirmed cases divided by all suspected, probable, and confirmed cases CEBS found. In Sierra Leone: sensitivity 16/53 = 30%, PPV 16/287 = 6%.

A fictional district: Over three months, community monitors raised alerts that led to 40 suspected, probable, or confirmed cases. Of these, 8 tested positive (confirmed). The national system, counting every source, confirmed 20 cases in the district in the same period.

Interpret: What does a low PPV mean for this system?

Takeaway: Community reporters can find cases early, but triggers only work when they are remembered and used. Judge a surveillance system by both sensitivity and positive predictive value, and by what it was designed to do.

Source read: Ratnayake, R., et al. (2016). Assessment of community event–based surveillance for Ebola virus disease, Sierra Leone, 2015. Emerging Infectious Diseases, 22(8), 1431–1437. Villages and numbers in the practice items are fictional.
