Week 7 Interactive: Cybercrime Prevention and Privacy

Week 7 objective: Match cybersecurity measures to common threats and identify when the Fourth Amendment generally requires a warrant for digital evidence.

Spotting phishing

Phishing tries to obtain logins or financial information by posing as a trusted organization, often by creating urgency or fear. The safest response is to verify the request by contacting the organization directly, not by clicking the link.

Layered defenses and legal limits

Organizations combine multi-factor authentication, prompt software updates, employee training (including phishing simulations), least-privilege access, and an incident response plan to identify, contain, and recover. Investigators remain bound by the Fourth Amendment: Riley v. California (2014) requires a warrant to search an arrested person's phone, United States v. Jones (2012) treats attaching a GPS tracker as a search, and Carpenter v. United States (2018) protects months of cell phone location records. Consent is an exception only when it is given voluntarily.

Takeaway: Most cyber incidents start with people, so verification, training, and layered defenses matter. When agencies investigate, digital evidence still gets Fourth Amendment protection: warrants for phones, GPS tracking, and long-term location data.
