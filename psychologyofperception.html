From Stimulus to Sensation:
The Psychology of Perception

🌟 Welcome to the Sensory Realm

Every second, millions of physical signals collide with your body: light waves, vibrations, floating chemicals. But how does raw physical energy transform into your personal, lived experience? This interactive breaks down the bridge between physical energy and psychological awareness into key stages: identifying physical stimuli, mapping them to specialized sensory receptors, separating biological hardware (sensation) from cognitive interpretation (perception), and exploring how your brain filters constant noise through sensory adaptation.

Sensed vs. Perceived

0/2 Answered

Sensing is passive and physical (what your biological hardware captures), while perceiving is active and psychological (how your cognitive software interprets that raw data into personal meaning based on stress, memories, or expectations).

Sensory Adaptation

Explore Timeline

To prevent cognitive overload, sensory receptors automatically decrease their firing rate during constant, unchanging exposure. As biological signals fade at the receptor level, your brain stops actively perceiving the stimulus, keeping your cognitive bandwidth open for new environmental changes.

Summary

A complete mental model links these mechanics together: physical energy in the world (stimulus) fires specialized cells (receptors) to gather biological data (sensation), which the brain translates into personal reality (perception) and filters out when constant (adaptation).

💡

Stimulus

Raw physical energy present in the environment (e.g., light waves, chemical molecules).

🔬

Sensory Receptors

Specialized cells (photoreceptors, hair cells, olfactory receptors, taste buds, mechanoreceptors) that detect physical stimuli.

🧠

Sensed vs. Perceived

Raw physical energy detected by biological hardware (sensation) vs. meaningful psychological interpretation by the brain (perception).

🌊

Sensory Adaptation

The automatic decrease in sensory sensitivity after constant, unchanging exposure to a stimulus.
