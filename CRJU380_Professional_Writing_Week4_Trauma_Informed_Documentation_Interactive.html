Week 4 Interactive: Trauma-Informed Documentation

Week 4 objective: Recognize how trauma can affect a person's account and write observations that are precise, neutral, and respectful.

Why this matters

A trauma-informed approach realizes how widespread trauma is, recognizes its signs, responds by building that knowledge into practice, and seeks to avoid retraumatizing people. Stress chemicals released during an attack can cause numbness, a flat or "drugged" appearance, or even laughter, and can scramble how memories are recorded. The memory may be correct but relayed in a confusing way. Reports that judge credibility by myths can derail investigations and harm survivors.

Practice 1: myth or fact?

Decide whether each statement is a Myth or a Fact according to this week's trauma-informed policing sources.

Practice 2: choose the best report line

Fictional call: Officer Ames takes a report from Jordan Price, 24, who says a coworker assaulted her in a parking garage.

For each observation, choose the line you would put in the report.

Takeaway: Confusion is not deception, and myths are not evidence. Describe behavior exactly as you saw and heard it, quote key statements, name who observed what, and leave out judgments about how a "real" victim should act. Precision and empathy work together.
